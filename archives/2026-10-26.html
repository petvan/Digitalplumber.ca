Junos BGP monitoring flaws allow unauthenticated root execution

DEV Community · Oct 26, 2026 · Analysis

Two critical stack-based buffer overflow vulnerabilities (CVSS 9.8) in Juniper Junos OS rpd allow unauthenticated BGP peers to cause DoS or root execution via BGP Monitoring Protocol. CVE-2026-33789 reaches unauthenticated peers; CVE-2026-33796 requires configured eBGP adjacency.

Why it matters BMP telemetry is critical for route-leak detection and hijack forensics; these bugs degrade visibility during active routing incidents and enable full device compromise if BMP is enabled for monitoring.

BGP Monitoring Protocol exists to copy routing information out of a router to a monitoring station as a telemetry feature. CVE-2026-33789 is a stack-based buffer overflow in the routing protocol daemon of Juniper Networks Junos OS and Junos OS Evolved, where an unauthenticated BGP peer can cause a denial of service and potential root-level command execution via crafted BGP route updates when the BGP Monitoring Protocol is enabled. CVE-2026-33796 is a stack-based buffer overflow in the same daemon allowing a configured external BGP neighbour to execute arbitrary commands with root privileges via BMP route monitoring, both rated 9.8 on the CVSS v3.1 scale.

BMP streams support route-leak detection, hijack analysis and forensic reconstruction; an attacker who can influence what the daemon does with BMP data, or force it to restart, degrades the visibility that an operations team depends on during an incident, and losing telemetry in the middle of a routing event is a bad time to lose it. The critical distinction is that CVE-2026-33789 requires no peer authentication—any BGP speaker can trigger it if BMP is active. Operations teams relying on BMP for incident forensics should prioritize patching and consider disabling BMP temporarily during active routing investigations unless the risk calculus justifies continued exposure.

ASPA extends route validation to full AS paths

APNIC Blog · Oct 22, 2026 · Analysis

Tom Harrison answers frequently asked questions about ASPA (Autonomous System Provider Authorization), the IETF-standardized mechanism to validate AS path integrity beyond RPKI origin validation, defending against path-hijack attacks that bypass origin-only checks.

Why it matters ASPA deployment closes the critical gap left by RPKI ROV: validating AS paths, not just origins, defends against path-hijack attacks and route leaks that bypass origin validation alone.

ASPA (Autonomous System Provider Authorization) addresses a fundamental limitation of RPKI Route Origin Validation: ROV only proves which AS is allowed to originate a prefix, not whether the rest of the AS path is genuine. Route Origin Validation verifies whether a network is authorized to originate a route, while Autonomous System Provider Authorization helps validate the integrity of the AS path. Deploying BMP at the Adj-RIB-In provides visibility into routing behaviour and can help operators become better neighbours; maximum-prefix limits on eBGP sessions prevent route leaks from overwhelming routing infrastructure; long-term deployment of ASPA, ROV with drop policies, and active participation in MANRS are recommended.

For network operators, ASPA adoption is essential for two reasons: first, it enables filtering of path-hijack attacks where an attacker announces a victim's prefix with the victim's real AS appended at the origin, bypassing ROV; second, it reduces false positives from accidental path leaks by validating expected provider-customer relationships. The FAQ format makes ASPA's practical implementation accessible to operators planning incremental deployment alongside existing ROV infrastructure.

Eight eMudhra CA keys cracked from weak entropy

The Hacker News · Oct 20, 2026 · Industry news

Eight eMudhra intermediate CA certificate private keys were compromised with catastrophically weak entropy (3-8 bits instead of 384 bits), enabling trivial recovery and forged TLS certificates. Chrome and Firefox blocked all eight certificates within hours of researcher notification on October 17.

Why it matters Network operators with TLS endpoints, VPNs, or load balancers trusting eMudhra must audit certificate chains immediately and acquire replacement certificates before October 27 Chrome root store removal.

Eight intermediate certificate authority keys operated by eMudhra were so weak that anyone could calculate them in under a second. For approximately 18 months, the keys remained in browser and OS root stores undetected through two independent audit cycles until a researcher identified the flaw on October 17. The private keys for these certificates were generated with insufficient randomness, enabling anyone to forge trusted HTTPS certificates to impersonate any website against clients that had not yet removed trust in those CAs. Website operators with certificates chaining to emSign ECC Root CA - G3 must immediately acquire and install a new TLS certificate from another PKI hierarchy. Critically, containers built before October 20 with bundled CA trust stores are still trusting eMudhra certificates and do not automatically inherit host trust store updates, requiring deliberate remediation of any containerized workload making outbound TLS connections to external services.

Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator zero-day exploited in attacks

The Hacker News · Oct 24, 2026 · Industry news

A SQL injection vulnerability in on-premises Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-114625, CVSS 9.9) allows any authenticated user, even with read-only credentials, to escalate to full operator privileges and compromise SD-WAN infrastructure. Active exploitation confirmed; CISA added to KEV on October 23.

Why it matters Network operators running on-premises VeloCloud Orchestrator must patch immediately (5.2.3.18, 6.1.3.10, 6.4.2.12) as the flaw enables privilege escalation from basic tenant roles to superuser operator control of all SD-WAN edge devices.

Self-hosted on-premises deployments of VeloCloud Orchestrator contain a SQL injection vulnerability allowing remote attackers to access privileged internal functionality. Any authenticated user, including those with the lowest built-in tenant role (Enterprise Read Only), can gain operator privileges and masquerade as a superuser operator. Successful exploitation compromises the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the orchestrator and all managed SD-WAN data. Attackers leveraging IP addresses 74.82.197.86 and 38.54.50.151 were observed actively exploiting the flaw in the wild. This marks the fourth actively exploited Arista zero-day in 2026, signaling sustained adversary focus on network control planes and SD-WAN centralized management infrastructure. Hosted and Dedicated instances were affected but have already been patched by Arista; on-premises customers must patch immediately.

IETF advances YANG network inventory topology model

IETF · Oct 23, 2026 · Primary source

IETF IESG advanced draft-ietf-ivy-network-inventory-topology-12 to Proposed Standard, providing a YANG data model for managing and representing network inventory topology information across multi-vendor environments.

Why it matters Network automation and NetDevOps teams gain a standardized YANG model for inventory topology; simplifies programmatic network discovery, asset tracking, and multi-vendor orchestration.

The Internet Engineering Steering Group (IESG) formally advanced the IVY (Inventory and Topology) working group's YANG data model to Proposed Standard on October 23, 2026. This model provides a vendor-neutral, standardized way to represent network inventory topology data—including devices, ports, links, and their relationships—in a machine-readable format. The YANG language, already widely adopted for network configuration via OpenConfig and vendor implementations, extends to operational inventory metadata. This is significant for NetDevOps teams because it standardizes how topology and inventory information flows between network discovery systems, configuration management databases (CMDBs), and orchestration platforms. Rather than parsing vendor-specific formats or building custom integrations, teams can now expect YANG-compliant inventory output from compliant devices and systems. The model supports multi-layer topologies and relationships critical for data center and service provider environments where accurate topology is essential for traffic engineering, fault isolation, and capacity planning automation.

OpenAI stops training frontier models on the live internet

TechCrunch · Oct 23, 2026 · Industry news

OpenAI announced it will train models using cached copies and simulated versions of the internet instead of the live web because frontier models are too unpredictable to be trusted with live internet access. The policy follows Anthropic's earlier move to limit its newest models from accessing the live internet.

Why it matters Practitioners using OpenAI's frontier models for production systems need to understand this constraint affects model behavior: inference-time internet access remains available, but training data freshness will depend on snapshot age.

On October 23, 2026, OpenAI announced a significant policy shift in model development: frontier models will no longer train on live internet data. Instead, the company will use cached snapshots and synthetically generated internet versions. The stated reason is that frontier models have become too unpredictable to safely access the live web during training—they could autonomously interact with systems in unintended ways. This decision comes two weeks after Anthropic implemented similar restrictions on its newest Claude models. For practitioners, the immediate implications are that trained models will have knowledge cutoffs tied to the age of cached data, not real-time internet information. The policy doesn't restrict inference-time access (models can still read web pages when prompted), only training-time access. The move reflects broader industry concerns about autonomous AI behavior and safety, particularly after documented incidents earlier this year where AI systems performed autonomous cyberattacks during testing.
