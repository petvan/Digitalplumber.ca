Oracle ships OCCL plugin for resilient AI training networks

HPCwire · Nov 10, 2026 · Primary source

Oracle Cloud Infrastructure released Oracle Collective Communications Library (OCCL), a network plugin for distributed AI workloads that handles congested paths, failed links, and NIC failures. Internal benchmarks show 2-10% increased average NCCL bus bandwidth for AllReduce operations on NVIDIA GB300 systems.

Why it matters OCCL supports multi-planar RDMA networks in vendor-agnostic fashion, directly addressing network-layer resilience as a bottleneck in multi-thousand-GPU training clusters where collective communication reliability determines job completion time.

Oracle's OCCL addresses a concrete pain point for practitioners running large-scale distributed AI training: network path failures and congestion that cause collective communication operations to stall entire training jobs. The library operates at the collective-communication transport layer, automatically rerouting traffic around link or NIC failures and restoring connectivity when hardware recovers. According to Oracle's benchmarks on 16-128 node NVIDIA GB300 clusters with four GPUs per node, OCCL increased average NCCL bus bandwidth by 2-10% for AllReduce operations. The plugin uses a vendor-agnostic network plugin interface, supporting both single-planar (traditional spine-leaf) and multi-planar RDMA topologies. This fills a gap in the collective-communication stack: while frameworks continue using NCCL/RCCL, OCCL injects networking intelligence for congestion control and failure recovery directly into the transport layer. For operators running large-scale training, this reduces operational friction of keeping distributed clusters healthy across hundreds or thousands of nodes where any single path failure cascades to all coordinating GPUs.

Broadcom says network performance now limits trillion-parameter AI training

HPCwire · Nov 11, 2026 · Vendor release

Broadcom announced at SC26 that network performance has become the limiting factor for trillion-parameter AI models and 100,000+ accelerator clusters. Company showcased open Ethernet fabric architecture using Tomahawk 6 and Jericho 4 silicon under ESUN standards as alternative to proprietary InfiniBand.

Why it matters Broadcom's 102.4 Tbps Tomahawk 6 platform provides open, multi-vendor alternative to NVIDIA's InfiniBand monopoly, enabling 100,000+ GPU cluster deployments at lower cost and without proprietary feature locks like SHARP.

Broadcom's SC26 positioning marks critical inflection point: shift from proprietary single-vendor stacks toward open, multi-vendor Ethernet fabrics for frontier-scale clusters. The company showcased integration of Tomahawk 6 (102.4 Tbps), Tomahawk Ultra (sub-250ns latency scale-up), and Jericho 4 switching silicon across complete rack-to-fabric hierarchies. Key technical contribution: cognitive routing (second generation) providing dynamic packet steering that adapts to traffic patterns, and Global Load Balancing as foundations for AI scale-out. Broadcom is chairing SC26's first workshop dedicated to high-performance fabrics for AI/HPC, signaling industry standardization momentum. Company announced third-generation co-packaged optics (CPO) platforms integrating optical engines directly into switch packages, reducing power consumption versus pluggable-optic approaches. For practitioners: this ecosystem push against NVIDIA's InfiniBand monopoly creates real optionality for teams deploying 100,000+ accelerator clusters. Open Ethernet avoids proprietary feature locks like NVIDIA SHARP (in-switch collective offload). Cost differences are material: Meta benchmarks show ~$650K for Ultra Ethernet vs. ~$1.2M for InfiniBand on 1024-GPU B200 clusters. However, open Ethernet remains less battle-tested at frontier scale compared to InfiniBand's production deployments on xAI Colossus and other verified 100K+ GPU systems.

Attackers exploit SonicWall SSL-VPN flaw to bypass MFA

CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog · Nov 6, 2026 · Primary source

CISA added high-severity CVE-2026-19868 (CVSS 7.1/7.5) to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog after SonicWall disclosed evidence of real-world exploitation. The flaw in SonicOS SSL-VPN allows users with valid alternate authentication credentials to bypass SAML authentication controls and SAML-enforced MFA. Fix available in SonicOS 7.3.4-7013 (Gen7) and 8.2.3-8015 (Gen8); SonicWall confirms no workarounds exist.

Why it matters An attacker with valid alternate-auth credentials can bypass the organization's identity provider (Okta, Entra ID) MFA check, allowing VPN login with password alone. This breaks zero-trust network access for organizations relying on SAML MFA as primary VPN defense; patch deadline is November 20 for federal agencies.

CVE-2026-19868 (CWE-308) fails to enforce SAML controls when users supply credentials for an alternate authentication method; if MFA depends on SAML, that enforcement is also bypassed. This is not unauthenticated—attackers need valid credentials first, but valid local or alternate-auth passwords are sufficient to bypass MFA. SonicWall published the advisory November 6; CISA added it to KEV the same day, confirming active exploitation. Federal agencies must patch by November 20. This directly undermines zero-trust architecture where VPN MFA is the perimeter control, making it a critical priority for any organization running SonicWall SSL-VPN with SAML-based MFA.

Founded in early 2026 by Yuval Zacharia, Aner Morag, and Boris Danilovich—security researchers from military and Preempt Security backgrounds—Bay already has ~35 employees and operational deployments across health, finance, and tech sectors. The $50M round combines $37M Series A (Battery Ventures, leading) with earlier $13M seed (YL Ventures, Striker Venture Partners), plus strategic investors including Micron Ventures, M12, and Microsoft's venture fund. RFC 10082 defines hybrid post-quantum signatures for CMS

RFC Editor · Nov 7, 2026 · Primary source

RFC 10082 published November 7, 2026 as Standards Track Proposed Standard. Defines combinations of ML-DSA with RSA, ECDSA, and EdDSA for use in Cryptographic Message Syntax (CMS), enabling hybrid post-quantum signatures in S/MIME.

Why it matters Post-quantum cryptography migration for S/MIME and code signing now has concrete standard; PKI and certificate infrastructure teams must evaluate implications for document and code signing workflows.

Composite ML-DSA pairs the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm with traditional signature algorithms (RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA). Specifies three ML-DSA variants—ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65, ML-DSA-87—each paired with specific traditional algorithms and digest functions. ML-DSA-65 with ECDSA P-256 uses SHA-512; ML-DSA-87 with Ed448 uses SHAKE256. Companion to RFC 10073 (X.509 encoding), enabling hybrid post-quantum signatures in S/MIME, code signing, and document signing. The Composite ML-DSA AlgorithmIdentifier must omit parameters field entirely. Implementation is in early stages; COTS libraries are still adding support. This establishes the first standards-track path for production hybrid PQC in messaging systems.

itechguides.com · Nov 10, 2026 · Analysis

Prohibitions, general-purpose AI obligations and Article 50 transparency requirements entered into force as of 10 November 2026. Registration of high-risk systems began in November 2026 with a 72-hour incident reporting window and penalties reaching $1 million for first violations and $3 million for repeat violations.

Why it matters Enterprise compliance with EU AI Act implementation now determines whether systems can stay online and whether enterprises can continue operating within EU jurisdictions.

The EU AI Act's enforcement timeline has moved from theory to mandatory compliance. As of November 10, 2026, three key enforcement layers are now active: prohibited-practice rules (in force since February 2025), general-purpose AI provider documentation and copyright-policy requirements (in force since August 2025), and Article 50 transparency duties requiring disclosure when users interact with AI systems and marking of synthetic content (in force as of August 2026). The Digital Omnibus amendment (July 27, 2026) simplified certain SME and startup compliance burdens. Registration for high-risk systems began in November 2026 with a mandatory 72-hour incident-reporting window. Penalties are substantial: €1 million for first violations, €3 million for repeat violations. By December 2, 2026, machines will require machine-readable marking and new restrictions on non-consensual intimate material and child sexual abuse material generators take effect. Enterprises in scope must have completed conformity-assessment documentation before deadlines; late compliance now carries enforcement risk.
