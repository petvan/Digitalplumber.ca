WhiteFiber links GPU clusters 83 km apart at 136 Tbps

Scale-across GPU clusters over dark fiber change data center design assumptions, showing 83 km separation is workable at 0.9 ms.

SDxCentral · Sep 23, 2026

WhiteFiber Continuum represents the first commercially available distributed GPU supercluster architecture, addressing a longstanding infrastructure ceiling: geographic distance as a hard limit on cluster scope. The system links two HITRUST-certified QTS facilities using 12 dark fiber strands from Zayo, with DriveNets providing the high-performance AI fabric (ethernet-based with deep buffering for scale-across connectivity) and WEKA providing NeuralMesh storage and memory infrastructure across both sites. The 136 Tbps / 0.9ms latency specification allows geographically separated GPUs to operate as local, enabling training jobs to span data centers without GPU stall. For practitioners, this solves the practical problem of power and space constraints at single campuses—the architecture is designed to extend, allowing additional sites to join via the same networking topology without reinvestment. It includes no single points of failure and maintains data sovereignty for regulated workloads. The system has submitted patent applications for the underlying implementation. Customer scale performance is still being demonstrated as final fiber testing continues.

Over half of enterprises let AI agents fix network faults

Network teams are handing more control to AI agents that don't just flag problems anymore — they actually fix them. The average organization generates about 4,100 monitoring alerts a day, more than half of them network-related, and clearing that backlog manually would take roughly 100 IT specialists working around the clock. The research underscores why autonomous NetOps is now table stakes for enterprise operations.

Cisco & Omdia Research · Sep 23, 2026

Cisco · Omdia · AIOps · Network Automation · Autonomous Agents

Cisco and Omdia's research reveals that more than half of enterprises have already deployed AI agents with autonomous remediation capabilities on their networks—a dramatic acceleration from pilots to production. The finding is grounded in operational reality: organizations are drowning in alert noise (4,100+ daily alerts per organization, >50% network-related) that human teams cannot triage at scale. This drives adoption of agents that not only detect anomalies but autonomously execute fixes—from configuration changes to traffic steering to incident containment. The research also notes that AI-driven traffic is accelerating (pace to double every six months), adding both load and complexity to networks even as AI agents help manage that complexity. For network teams, this marks a permanent shift from "monitoring and alerting" to "monitoring, detecting, and remediating" as the operational baseline. The economic pressure is clear: hiring enough engineers to manually clear that backlog is infeasible, making autonomous agents the only viable path to scale.

Security

AI agent chain steals 600,000 credit cards for $25 a target

Gambit researchers identified a campaign in which a threat actor gave short instructions to a chain of three open-source AI agent frameworks and let automation carry out the rest of the attack with minimal ongoing supervision. The cost per target dropped to ~$25, eliminating traditional economic barriers. A campaign that once required skilled operators now runs autonomously on open-source frameworks.

BleepingComputer / Gambit Threat Research · Sep 23, 2026

AI Security · Agent Frameworks · E-Commerce · Threat Intelligence · Autonomous Attacks

Gambit threat intelligence documented a real-world autonomous attack campaign in which threat actors chained three open-source AI agent frameworks (Strix for reconnaissance, Cairn for exploitation, Hermes for orchestration) to conduct reconnaissance, exploitation, and skimming deployment against 100+ e-commerce sites with minimal human direction. The campaign resulted in theft of 600,000+ credit card numbers. What makes this significant for practitioners: the attack economics have fundamentally changed. Previously, attacking a low-value target required similar human effort as attacking a high-value one, creating a natural filter. With AI agents handling reconnaissance through exploitation through deployment, the cost per target dropped to ~$25, eliminating that economic barrier. Organizations can no longer assume attackers will skip low-value sites. The attack chain moved from reconnaissance to exploitation to skimmer deployment to cleanup in single engagement windows per target—speed that human operators cannot match. Detection strategies that rely on noticing slow, human-paced intrusions miss these fast automated campaigns entirely. This signals a permanent shift in attacker economics and required defense architectures.

Orange to trial Nokia AI-RAN platform on live network

Orange is preparing to test Nokia's AI-RAN platform in its live network, putting claimed radio-efficiency gains to the commercial test while exploring the bigger proposition of shared compute for AI workloads. Orange will start small, testing performance, energy efficiency, operational impact and total cost of ownership before scaling, considering all of Nokia's deployment models—AirScale plug-ins, standalone AI-RAN nodes, and cloud-native COTS deployments. This represents the inflection point between laboratory trials and production-grade validation where AI-RAN must prove itself against real traffic, paying customers, and operational complexity.

RCR Wireless · Sep 22, 2026

Orange · Nokia · AI-RAN · Live network trials · Radio access networks

Orange is preparing to test Nokia's AI-RAN platform in its live network, putting claimed radio-efficiency gains to the commercial test while exploring shared compute for AI workloads. This is a significant step beyond the numerous lab trials Nokia has announced. The trial methodology is pragmatic: Orange will start small, testing performance, energy efficiency, operational impact and total cost of ownership before scaling, and will consider all of Nokia's deployment models—AirScale plug-ins, standalone AI-RAN nodes, and cloud-native COTS deployments.

The broader context matters for practitioners: Orange sees potential in sensing, positioning, and other AI applications, but the economics of putting spare 'fallow compute' to work remain unproven. For operators evaluating AI-RAN investments, this Orange deployment is an important litmus test—it will clarify whether the claimed 20% spectral efficiency gains and energy benefits hold in production networks carrying real subscriber traffic. The multi-deployment-model approach signals Orange's unwillingness to be locked into a single architecture, suggesting negotiations around flexibility and interoperability remain open.
