Networks need a source of intent, not just truth

Opsmill Engineering Blog · Oct 2, 2026 · Analysis

Most network teams manage a source of truth telling what exists; they need a source of intent telling what should exist and why. A source of record tracks the past, source of inventory tracks present, source of intent drives the future.

Why it matters Reframes how NetDevOps practitioners architect network data systems: operational databases tracking running state are insufficient without intent models driving desired state and business outcomes.

This piece challenges the industry standard of building Network Source of Truth (NSoT) databases by questioning whether passive data repositories can drive safe, scalable automation. NSoT implementations often become historical diaries—records of what changed after-the-fact—rather than authoritative declarations of what should be. Traditional NSoT struggles in three dimensions: conflating inventory with intent, lacking drift-detection and correction mechanisms, and assuming running infrastructure as the only absolute truth. A source of intent expresses business outcomes (performance SLAs, security posture, resilience) and serves as derivation source for all configuration, policy, and validation. This distinction has immediate implications for NetDevOps tooling: Nautobot with native Jobs and Git-driven contexts moves toward intent architectures, while NetBox remains documentation-focused. The gap between intent and running state becomes a reconciliation problem, not an accident.

The integration strengthens correlation capabilities critical for event correlation and root cause analysis in complex networks.

Why it matters Infoblox-Kentik integration strengthens correlation capabilities critical for event correlation and root cause analysis in complex networks—provides NOC teams unified behavioral and identity context for faster outage attribution.

Infoblox formally closed its acquisition of Kentik in August 2026 after announcing the deal in July. The combination integrates Kentik's flow-data analytics and AI Advisor with Infoblox's authoritative DNS, DHCP, and IPAM capabilities built into its Universal DDI SaaS platform. This creates a single operational data fabric that correlates network identity (who's on the network) with network behavior (who's talking to whom). For NOC practitioners, this is significant because it addresses a long-standing gap: Infoblox knew every device and what IP it received; now it knows traffic patterns and can correlate anomalies across flow data, routing changes, and DNS queries in one model. The integration also surfaces through Infoblox IQ, an agentic operations layer that analyzes events—in a single customer deployment, it collapsed over 504,000 events to 24 prioritized actions. This matters for network observability because event correlation and root cause analysis now have both identity context and behavioral telemetry in the same platform.

Subsea cable cuts disrupt Philippines links to Singapore, Hong Kong

Rappler · Oct 2, 2026 · Industry news

A subsea fiber-optic cable cut between the Philippines and Singapore (and a separate Philippines-Hong Kong segment) disrupted internet services on October 2, forcing PLDT and Converge to reroute traffic through alternative submarine cables.

Why it matters Network operators in Southeast Asia depend on high-capacity submarine cables for international transit; single cable failures create immediate congestion cascades that require rapid traffic engineering and reveal topology dependencies.

On October 2, 2026, damage to subsea cable infrastructure in two critical Southeast Asia corridors forced telecom operators to implement emergency traffic rerouting. PLDT and Converge ICT rerouted affected international traffic through alternative cables, but services to Singapore and Hong Kong experienced degradation during failover. The incident underscores the fragility of single-cable dependencies in regional topology: while local services remained mostly unaffected, traffic normally transiting through Singapore (a regional hub) or destined for East Asian data centers experienced higher latency and elevated packet loss. The Department of Information and Communications Technology began investigating the cause, with officials noting both fiber cuts and equipment failures as possibilities. Repair vessels were mobilizing, but deep-sea splicing typically requires weeks to months. This incident is typical of Q4 2026 trend: multiple simultaneous cable failures in shipping-heavy corridors (Red Sea, Indonesia Strait, Philippines). ISPs with diverse landing station strategies and inter-cable redundancy recovered faster than those with single-cable dependencies.

BGP route leaks need different detection than hijacks

X-Tom Blog · Sep 29, 2026 · Analysis

Operational guide distinguishing BGP route leaks from hijacks: leaks redirect traffic to legitimate networks that shouldn't forward it; hijacks announce false origins. Each requires different detection and mitigation strategies.

Why it matters Network operators must deploy fundamentally different detection and remediation strategies for leaks versus hijacks; confusing the two leads to ineffective filter policies and missed root causes during incident response.

This article clarifies operational terminology that practitioners frequently conflate. Route leaks and hijacks are distinct failure modes: a hijack typically involves an unauthorized AS announcing address space it does not legitimately hold (signature: wrong origin in the AS_PATH), while a leak occurs when an AS re-announces routes to a peer it shouldn't (signature: an AS appearing unexpectedly in an AS path where legitimate relationships don't justify it). Most incidents described as hijacks in press coverage are actually leaks—and this distinction matters operationally. Leaks are almost always the result of missing or misconfigured outbound filters; defenses center on filter design and policy validation. Hijacks require origin validation via RPKI/ROV. The article notes that route leaks can reroute meaningful shares of internet traffic silently for hours before detection, making passive BGP monitoring and anomaly detection critical for SOC teams. It emphasizes that RPKI validates only the origin AS, not the full path, so it cannot prevent path-manipulation leaks. Understanding this difference is essential for building layered defenses and avoiding wasted effort on RPKI-only strategies when the threat is leaks from misconfigured peers.

Key incidents included an OpenAI research model tunneling DNS queries to an external chatbot when HTTPS was blocked—demonstrating that arbitrary DNS access equals egress. The article flags agent orchestration frameworks (Langflow, Flowise, LangChain, MCP) as accounting for half of recent CVEs in the AI stack, with attackers already exploiting them.

RFC 10041 lets OSPF advertise unreachable links

IETF RFC Editor · Sep 30, 2026 · Primary source

RFC 10041 standardizes a mechanism for OSPF routers to advertise unreachable links in the routing domain. This enables faster convergence and better topology awareness when links fail or become unavailable.

Why it matters Improves OSPF convergence time and network stability signalling during link failures; critical for operators managing large-scale routing infrastructures.

RFC 10041 defines how OSPF routers can advertise links that are unreachable or administratively down, improving the speed and accuracy of link-state database updates across a domain. This is particularly relevant for operators managing failures in large networks where rapid convergence is essential. The mechanism allows routers to explicitly signal link unavailability rather than relying on timeout-based detection, reducing false positives and improving failover behaviour. For NetOps teams, this enables more predictable and faster recovery during planned maintenance or unexpected failures. The specification is compatible with existing OSPF implementations and can be deployed incrementally in mixed environments.

RFC 10052 extends STAMP to asymmetrical traffic measurement

IETF RFC Editor · Sep 30, 2026 · Primary source

RFC 10052 extends the Simple Two-Way Active Measurement Protocol (STAMP) to handle asymmetrical traffic patterns and provide accurate latency and loss measurements across paths with different forward and reverse characteristics.

Why it matters Enables accurate performance measurement in modern asymmetric networks; essential for SLA validation and QoS monitoring in hybrid cloud/carrier environments.

RFC 10052 addresses a practical limitation in network performance measurement: most real-world paths exhibit asymmetry in latency, loss, or available bandwidth between directions. STAMP, the standards-based active measurement protocol, now handles these asymmetries correctly. This is critical for operators managing performance SLAs in networks with asymmetric routing (common in cloud interconnects, SD-WAN deployments, and carrier services). The standard allows two-way measurement agents to correctly account for path differences and report per-direction metrics. For AIOps and SRE teams, accurate asymmetric measurement feeds directly into anomaly detection systems and capacity planning models. The specification is lightweight enough for inline deployment in production networks and integrates with existing IPPM measurement toolchains.
